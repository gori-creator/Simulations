## What is it about?

In base ten every place is worth **ten times** the place to its right: 10 ones make a ten, 10 tens make a hundred, and so on. The value of a digit depends on its place – in $4{,}253{,}718$ the $5$ stands for $50{,}000$. Large numbers are read in **groups of three**: ones, thousands, millions, billions.

$$
4{,}253{,}718 = 4 \cdot 1{,}000{,}000 + 2 \cdot 100{,}000 + 5 \cdot 10{,}000 + 3 \cdot 1{,}000 + 7 \cdot 100 + 1 \cdot 10 + 8
$$

**Rounding:** look at the digit to the right of the rounding place. $0$–$4$: round down, $5$–$9$: round up. On the number line the rounded number is the **nearer neighbour**; exactly halfway is rounded up by convention.

## Try it

1. Load [999,999](?n=999999&r=5) and tap **+** in the ones column: ten counters are regrouped into one counter of the next place – again and again.
2. Load [1,000](?n=1000&r=1) and tap **−** in the ones column. Where does the counter come from?
3. Drag the black pin across the halfway mark. When does the rounding arc switch sides?
4. Tap a column heading to choose the rounding place and watch the magnifier zoom.

## Notes for teachers

- The counters in the place value chart connect to primary school; regrouping and exchanging are animated and named. Tap a column heading to set the rounding place, tap a digit card to see its value.
- The digits $0$–$9$ under the magnified number line show that the deciding digit tells you in which tenth of the interval the number lies.
- Turn off “Show the rounded number right away” (`show=0`) for prediction tasks.
- **Note:** the German *Milliarde* is the English *billion* ($10^9$); the German *Billion* is $10^{12}$.
