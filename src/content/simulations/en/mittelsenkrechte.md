## What is it about?

The **perpendicular bisector** $m$ of a segment $AB$ passes through the midpoint $M$ of $AB$ at right angles. Every point $P$ on $m$ is equally far from $A$ and $B$ ($PA = PB$) – and only these points. A line consisting of exactly the points with a given property is called a **locus**. Reason: reflecting in $m$ maps $A$ onto $B$ and keeps every point of $m$ fixed.

The **angle bisector** $w$ splits an angle $\alpha$ into two angles of $\frac{\alpha}{2}$. Every point on $w$ is equally far from both arms (measured perpendicularly) – and inside the angle only these points.

**Constructions:** For $m$, draw arcs with the **same radius** (more than half of $AB$) around $A$ and $B$ and join their intersections $S_1$ and $S_2$. For $w$, an arc around the vertex $S$ cuts the arms at $P_1$ and $P_2$; equal arcs around $P_1$ and $P_2$ meet at $W$, and $SW$ is the bisector because $SP_1WP_2$ is a kite.

**Outlook:** The three perpendicular bisectors of a triangle meet at the circumcentre $U$, which is equally far from all three vertices.

## Tasks

### Task 1: Too small

Tim wants to bisect a $6\,\text{cm}$ segment and sets his compasses to $2.5\,\text{cm}$. What happens?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

The arcs do not meet, since $2.5 + 2.5 < 6$. The radius has to be more than $3\,\text{cm}$.

</details>

## Notes for teachers

- **Use:** year 7 (Bavaria: Lernbereich 2.1, symmetric figures and basic constructions). Good for discovering loci, for demonstrating the constructions on a projector and for practice. The circumcircle is an outlook.
- **Model:** squared paper with $0.5\,\text{cm}$ per square; $P$ snaps to grid points and onto the bisector when close. Rounded lengths are shown with “≈”.
- **Misconceptions:** any perpendicular to $AB$ is taken for the bisector; the compasses are changed between the two arcs; distances to an arm are measured at a slant; the circumcentre is thought to lie always inside the triangle.
