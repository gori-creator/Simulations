## What is it about?

Light travels in **straight lines**. Behind an opaque object there is a region that receives no light – its **shadow**. **Edge rays** from the rim of the light source past the rim of the object mark the borders of the shadow.

- A **point-like source** gives a sharp shadow: **umbra** only.
- An **extended source** gives an umbra (no part of the lamp is visible) surrounded by a **penumbra** (part of the lamp is visible) – the edge becomes blurred.
- **Two lamps** give two shadows; where they overlap there is umbra. A red and a green lamp light the screen **yellow**; the shadow of the red lamp is green and vice versa.

For a point-like lamp the size of the shadow follows from similar triangles:

$$
\frac{S}{G} = \frac{b}{a}
$$

with $a$ the distance lamp–object and $b$ the distance lamp–screen.

**Eclipses:** at **new moon** the Moon’s umbra can reach the Earth (total solar eclipse, partial in the penumbra, annular if the umbra falls short). At **full moon** the Moon can enter the Earth’s shadow (lunar eclipse); in the umbra it glows copper red.

## Tasks

### Task 1: Size of the shadow

A point-like lamp is at $10\,\text{cm}$, a disc with $G = 8\,\text{cm}$ at $30\,\text{cm}$ and the screen at $90\,\text{cm}$. How large is the shadow? [Load task](?lx=10&ox=30&od=8&sx=90&_hide=1)

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

$a = 20\,\text{cm}$, $b = 80\,\text{cm}$, so $S = 8\,\text{cm} \cdot \frac{80}{20} = 32\,\text{cm}$.

</details>

### Task 2: Twice as large

The lamp is at $0\,\text{cm}$ and the screen at $80\,\text{cm}$. Where must the disc be for a shadow twice its size? [Load task](?lx=0&sx=80&ox=20&_hide=1)

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

$\frac{b}{a} = 2$ gives $a = 40\,\text{cm}$ – halfway between lamp and screen. [Show](?lx=0&sx=80&ox=40)

</details>

### Task 3: Why not every month?

In the simulation every new moon causes a solar eclipse. Why are real eclipses much rarer?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

The Moon’s orbit is tilted by about $5^\circ$ against the Earth’s orbit, so the Moon’s shadow usually passes above or below the Earth. The simulation shows the orbits from above and leaves out the tilt.

</details>

## Notes for teachers

- Lamp and object are modelled as discs parallel to the screen; holders cast no shadow. The eclipse model is schematic and not to scale (the Sun and the Moon look about equally large from the Earth, as in reality).
- **Misconceptions:** “The shadow gets smaller when the object moves towards the lamp.” – It gets larger. “The phases of the Moon are caused by the Earth’s shadow.” – Only a lunar eclipse is.
