## What is it about?

In the **coordinate plane** a point is given by two numbers. The horizontal axis is the **x-axis**, the vertical one the **y-axis**; they meet at the **origin** $O(0, 0)$. For $P(3, 5)$ the **x-coordinate** comes first: from the origin go **3 to the right**, then **5 up**. Swapping the numbers gives a different point, $(5, 3)$.

| Name | Endpoints | Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| segment AB | A and B | can be measured |
| ray AB | only A (starting point) | endless |
| line AB | none | endless |

Two lines $g$ and $h$ that meet at a right angle are **perpendicular** ($g \perp h$); lines that never meet are **parallel** ($g \parallel h$). With a set square: put the edge on $g$. If $h$ lies on the centre line, $h \perp g$; if $h$ runs along one of the thin lines parallel to the edge, $h \parallel g$.

The **distance** of a point $P$ from a line $g$ is the length of the perpendicular from $P$ to $g$ – the shortest path.

A **circle** consists of all points at the same distance $r$ (the **radius**) from its **centre** $M$; the **diameter** is $d = 2 \cdot r$. Comparing the distance from $M$ to a line with $r$ tells you whether the line misses the circle (greater), touches it at one point – a **tangent** – (equal), or cuts it at two points – a **secant** – (smaller). A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.

## Notes for teachers

- **Use:** grade 5, introducing the coordinate plane (first quadrant), parallel, perpendicular, distance and tangent; the point game works well as a warm-up.
- **Model:** all points lie on grid points; one unit is 1 cm. Perpendicular, parallel and tangent are decided exactly; lengths are rounded to millimetres like a ruler reading. The set square is shown at half size.
- **Misconceptions:** swapping x and y (the game detects this), “perpendicular” meaning “vertical”, measuring a distance along a slanted line instead of at right angles.
