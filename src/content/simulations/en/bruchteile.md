## What is it about?

A **fraction** $\frac{z}{n}$ describes a part of a whole:

- The **denominator** $n$ tells you into how many **equal** parts the whole is cut.
- The **numerator** $z$ tells you how many of these parts you take.

So $\frac{3}{4}$ of a pizza means: cut the pizza into 4 equal slices and take 3 of them.

**Expanding** means cutting every piece again into $k$ equal parts. Then there are $k$ times as many pieces, and you take $k$ times as many – the amount stays the same:

$$
\frac{3}{4} = \frac{3 \cdot 2}{4 \cdot 2} = \frac{6}{8}
$$

**Reducing** is the reverse: divide numerator and denominator by the same number, $\frac{6}{8} = \frac{3}{4}$.

Every fraction can also be written as a **decimal**: $\frac{3}{4} = 0.75 = 75\,\%$. Some decimals are **recurring**, for example $\frac{1}{3} = 0.\overline{3}$.

If the numerator is greater than the denominator, you need more than one whole: $\frac{7}{4} = 1\frac{3}{4}$.

A fraction can also refer to a **quantity**. $\frac{3}{4}$ of €80 means: split €80 into 4 equal parts ($80 : 4 = 20$) and take 3 of them ($3 \cdot 20 = 60$). So $\frac{3}{4}$ of €80 is exactly €60.

### Exercise 3: Compare

Which is more: $\frac{2}{3}$ or $\frac{3}{4}$ of a pizza? Explain using a common denominator.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

$\frac{2}{3} = \frac{8}{12}$ and $\frac{3}{4} = \frac{9}{12}$. So $\frac{3}{4}$ is more – by $\frac{1}{12}$ of a pizza.

</details>

### Exercise 4: Recurring decimals

Write $\frac{1}{6}$ and $\frac{5}{6}$ as decimals.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

$\frac{1}{6} = 0.1\overline{6}$ and $\frac{5}{6} = 0.8\overline{3}$ – here one digit that does not repeat comes before the recurring part. [Show 5/6](?z=5&n=6&shape=strecke)

</details>

### Exercise 5: Fractions of quantities

Work out: a) $\frac{3}{4}$ of €80, b) $\frac{2}{5}$ of $35$ students, c) $\frac{2}{3}$ of $1$ hour. Picture the whole in the simulation.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

a) $80 : 4 = 20$, $3 \cdot 20 = 60$, so €60. [Pizza in quarters](?z=3&n=4)

b) $35 : 5 = 7$, $2 \cdot 7 = 14$, so $14$ students. [Line segment in fifths](?z=2&n=5&shape=strecke)

c) $1$ h $= 60$ min, $60 : 3 = 20$, $2 \cdot 20 = 40$, so $40$ min – like two thirds of a clock face. [Pizza in thirds](?z=2&n=3)

</details>

### Exercise 6: Special fractions

What do $\frac{0}{4}$, $\frac{4}{4}$ and $\frac{3}{1}$ mean? Set them and write them without a fraction bar.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

$\frac{0}{4} = 0$: no piece taken. [0/4](?z=0&n=4) $\frac{4}{4} = 1$: all pieces, so exactly one whole. [4/4](?z=4&n=4) $\frac{3}{1} = 3$: each whole stays uncut, you take 3 wholes. [3/1](?z=3&n=1)

</details>

## Ideas for the classroom

- **Use:** Bavaria, grade 6 (fractions and parts of a whole, expanding and reducing, decimals).
- **Operation:** Pieces can be tapped directly – good on an interactive whiteboard. The selected pieces are pulled out.
- **Common stumbling blocks:**
  - The parts must be **equal** in size – otherwise it is not a fraction of the whole.
  - "$\frac{6}{8}$ is more than $\frac{3}{4}$ because the numbers are bigger." – Expanding shows that it is the same amount.
  - "A bigger denominator means a bigger piece."
- **Fractions of quantities:** The simulation shows fractions of a whole. For quantities (money, time, numbers of people), let the whole stand for the quantity, for example the pizza for €80 or for 1 hour (exercise 5).
