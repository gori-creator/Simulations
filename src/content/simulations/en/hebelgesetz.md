## What is it about?

A **lever** is a rigid body that can turn about a **pivot**. A force acting on the lever tries to turn it. How strongly depends not only on the force $F$ but also on the **lever arm** $a$: the distance between the pivot and the **line of action** of the force. Their product is the **torque**:

$$
M = F \cdot a \qquad \text{unit: } 1\,\text{N} \cdot \text{m}
$$

**Law of the lever:** a lever is balanced when the anticlockwise and the clockwise torques are equal:

$$
F_1 \cdot a_1 = F_2 \cdot a_2
$$

A $50\,\text{g}$ weight has the weight force $F = 0.05\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{kg}} \approx 0.49\,\text{N}$. So three weights at $20\,\text{cm}$ balance two weights at $30\,\text{cm}$:

$$
1.47\,\text{N} \cdot 0.20\,\text{m} = 0.98\,\text{N} \cdot 0.30\,\text{m} \approx 0.29\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}
$$

Double the lever arm – half the force. If several forces act on one side, add their torques.

**Two-sided and one-sided levers:** in a **two-sided lever** the forces act on different sides of the pivot (seesaw, beam balance, scissors). In a **one-sided lever** both act on the same side and point in opposite directions (wheelbarrow, bottle opener, nutcracker). If we call one force the **load** $F_L$ with the **load arm** $a_L$ and the other the **effort** $F_K$ with the effort arm $a_K$, then in both cases

$$
F_K \cdot a_K = F_L \cdot a_L \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad F_K = F_L \cdot \frac{a_L}{a_K}
$$

(The indices K and L come from the German words *Kraft* and *Last*.)

**Pulling at an angle:** when you pull at an angle, what counts is not the distance from the pivot to the point where the force acts, but the distance to the line of action. You find it by drawing the perpendicular from the pivot to the line of action. This **effective lever arm** is shorter – you have to pull harder. (With trigonometry: $a = r \cdot \sin\beta$, $\beta$: angle between lever and direction of pull.)

**Golden rule of mechanics:** a long effort arm saves force – but the hand has to move further. When the lever turns, the distances behave like the arms: $\frac{s_K}{s_L} = \frac{a_K}{a_L}$. Therefore

$$
F_K \cdot s_K = F_L \cdot s_L
$$

“What you save in force you pay for in distance.” The lever does not reduce the work $W = F \cdot s$. A lever is a **force converter**.

## Tasks

### Task 1: Making it balance

Four weights hang at hole 3 on the left, i.e. $15\,\text{cm}$ from the pivot. Where must three weights hang? (Holes are $5\,\text{cm}$ apart.) [Load task](?n1=4&x1=-3&n2=3&x2=1&_hide=1)

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

$4 \cdot 0.49\,\text{N} \cdot 15\,\text{cm} = 3 \cdot 0.49\,\text{N} \cdot a_2$, so $a_2 = \frac{4 \cdot 15\,\text{cm}}{3} = 20\,\text{cm}$ – hole 4 on the right. [Show solution](?n1=4&x1=-3&n2=3&x2=4)

</details>

### Task 2: What does the spring balance read?

Six weights hang at hole 2 on the left. The spring balance pulls straight down at hole 8 on the right. What does it read when the lever is balanced? [Load task](?n1=6&x1=-2&n2=0&km=1&kx=8&kw=-90&kf=0&_hide=1)

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

$F_K = \frac{2.94\,\text{N} \cdot 0.10\,\text{m}}{0.40\,\text{m}} \approx 0.74\,\text{N}$. Four times the lever arm, a quarter of the force. [Check](?n1=6&x1=-2&n2=0&km=1&kx=8&kw=-90&kf=0.74)

</details>

### Task 3: One-sided lever

Four weights hang at hole 5 on the right, the spring balance pulls straight up at hole 9 on the right. Calculate the reading. [Load task](?n1=0&n2=4&x2=5&km=1&kx=9&kw=90&kf=0&_hide=1)

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

$F_K = \frac{1.96\,\text{N} \cdot 25\,\text{cm}}{45\,\text{cm}} \approx 1.09\,\text{N}$. In a one-sided lever the effort points in the opposite direction to the load. [Check](?n1=0&n2=4&x2=5&km=1&kx=9&kw=90&kf=1.09)

</details>

### Task 4: Pulling at an angle

Three weights hang at hole 4 on the left. At hole 6 on the right the spring balance pulls downwards, once straight down and once at $60^\circ$ to the horizontal. Why do you have to pull harder at an angle? Find the effective lever arm in the simulation. [Load task](?n1=3&x1=-4&n2=0&km=1&kx=6&kw=-60&kf=0.98)

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

Straight down, $0.98\,\text{N}$ is enough. At an angle the distance of the line of action from the pivot is only about $26\,\text{cm}$ instead of $30\,\text{cm}$, so you need $F_K = \frac{0.294\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}}{0.26\,\text{m}} \approx 1.13\,\text{N}$. [Check](?n1=3&x1=-4&n2=0&km=1&kx=6&kw=-60&kf=1.13)

</details>

### Task 5: Wheelbarrow

A wheelbarrow holds $80\,\text{kg}$ of sand. Its centre of gravity is $50\,\text{cm}$ from the wheel axle. The hands hold the handles $140\,\text{cm}$ from the axle. Which force is needed to hold it? What changes if the sand lies $30\,\text{cm}$ from the axle?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

$F_L = 80\,\text{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{kg}} \approx 785\,\text{N}$ and $F_K = 785\,\text{N} \cdot \frac{50}{140} \approx 280\,\text{N}$. With a load arm of $30\,\text{cm}$ only about $168\,\text{N}$ – heavy loads belong at the front, near the wheel. [Check](?mode=daily&obj=barrow&bm=80&bl=50) · [loaded at the front](?mode=daily&obj=barrow&bm=80&bl=30)

</details>

### Task 6: Golden rule with a nutcracker

Cracking a nut takes $300\,\text{N}$. The nut is $4\,\text{cm}$ from the hinge, the hand presses $16\,\text{cm}$ from the hinge. How large is the force of the hand? How far does the hand have to move if the nut has to be squeezed by $1\,\text{mm}$?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

$F_K = 300\,\text{N} \cdot \frac{4}{16} = 75\,\text{N}$. The hand moves four times as far as the point at the nut, i.e. $4\,\text{mm}$. Work: $75\,\text{N} \cdot 4\,\text{mm} = 300\,\text{N} \cdot 1\,\text{mm}$. [Check](?mode=daily&obj=nut&nl=4&nk=16)

</details>

## Ideas for the classroom

- **Use:** levers and the law of the lever, torque, force converters and the golden rule of mechanics in lower secondary. The simulation uses the terms torque, effort arm and load arm; some textbooks only speak of the lever arm and state the law of the lever without torque. “Show torques” (`mom=0`) and “Show lever arms” (`arms=0`) let you hide these aids, e.g. for your own series of measurements.
- **Operation:** weights are dragged from the box onto a hole; stacks can be moved and made smaller by tapping. The ring of the spring balance sets the direction and the force. Everything can also be set with the sliders.
- **Model:** weights of $50\,\text{g}$, $g = 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{kg}}$, holes $5\,\text{cm}$ apart. If your class uses $g \approx 10\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{kg}}$, one weight is $0.5\,\text{N}$ and results differ slightly from the simulation. The lever is supported at its centre of gravity, so its own weight produces no torque. A pointer with a small counterweight below the pivot makes a balanced lever return to horizontal (stable equilibrium); otherwise it tips to the stop. Very small differences (below $0.003\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$, e.g. from rounding the spring balance reading) are treated as friction in the pivot. The timing of the motion is only qualitative. The spring balance has a range of $5\,\text{N}$ and keeps its direction of pull when the lever tips.
- **Everyday levers:** the force holding the bottle cap and the force needed to crack the nut are freely chosen example values, not measurements. The weights of the wheelbarrow and the tools are neglected; the forces act perpendicular to the lever. For simplicity the seesaw also settles horizontally when balanced.
- **Common stumbling blocks:**
  - “The heavier side always goes down.” – What matters is the torque, not the force alone.
  - “A lever saves work.” – It saves force, but you have to move further.
  - “For an oblique pull the distance to the point of application counts.” – The distance of the line of action from the pivot counts.
  - “The law of the lever does not hold for a one-sided lever.” – It holds just the same; effort and load simply point in opposite directions.
