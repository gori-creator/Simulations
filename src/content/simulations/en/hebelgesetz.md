## What is it about?

A **lever** is a rigid body that can turn about a **pivot**. How strongly a force turns it depends on the force $F$ and on the **lever arm** $a$, the distance between the pivot and the **line of action** of the force. Their product is the **torque**:

$$
M = F \cdot a \qquad \text{unit: } 1\,\text{N} \cdot \text{m}
$$

**Law of the lever:** a lever is balanced when the anticlockwise and the clockwise torques are equal:

$$
F_1 \cdot a_1 = F_2 \cdot a_2
$$

A $50\,\text{g}$ weight has a weight force of about $0.49\,\text{N}$, so three weights at $20\,\text{cm}$ balance two weights at $30\,\text{cm}$. In a **one-sided lever** (wheelbarrow, bottle opener, nutcracker) load and effort act on the same side of the pivot in opposite directions; the law still holds: $F_K \cdot a_K = F_L \cdot a_L$.

When you pull **at an angle**, only the perpendicular distance of the line of action from the pivot counts – the effective lever arm is shorter and you need more force.

**Golden rule of mechanics:** a long effort arm saves force, but the hand must move further: $F_K \cdot s_K = F_L \cdot s_L$. The work done is not reduced.

## Notes for teachers

- The weights are $50\,\text{g}$ each, $g = 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{kg}}$, holes $5\,\text{cm}$ apart. A pointer with a small counterweight below the pivot makes a balanced lever return to horizontal; otherwise it tips to the stop. Tiny differences below $0.003\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ are treated as friction in the pivot. The motion is only qualitative.
- The force needed to lift the bottle cap and to crack the nut are example values. The weights of the wheelbarrow and the tools are neglected.
- **Misconceptions:** “the heavier side always goes down” (torque matters), “a lever saves work” (it saves force, not work), “for an oblique pull the distance to the point of application counts” (the line of action counts).
