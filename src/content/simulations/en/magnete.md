## What is it about?

Every magnet has two **poles**: a **north pole** (usually red in schools) and a **south pole** (usually green). If you hold two magnets close together:

- **Unlike poles attract** (north pole and south pole).
- **Like poles repel** (north and north, south and south).

The force quickly becomes smaller when the magnets are further apart. Both magnets feel forces of equal size in opposite directions.

**Which materials are attracted?** Only **iron, nickel and cobalt** (and materials that contain them, for example steel). A piece of iron is attracted by **either** pole. Close to the magnet it briefly becomes a magnet itself, in such a way that the unlike pole faces the magnet. Other metals such as aluminium, copper or gold are not attracted.

**The magnetic field** is the space around a magnet in which forces act. You cannot see it, but you can make it visible:

- **Iron filings** turn into tiny magnets and line up.
- **Compass needles** are small magnets that can turn. Their north pole points in the direction of the field.

This gives the **field line pattern**: the field lines **leave at the north pole** and **enter at the south pole**. Where they are close together, the field is strong. Field lines never cross.

**Elementary magnets:** you can imagine iron as made of many tiny magnets. If they are disordered, they cancel each other out. If you stroke a nail several times in the same direction with a magnet, they line up: the nail becomes a magnet itself. Hammering or strong heating (above $768\,^\circ\text{C}$ for iron) mixes up the order again. If you break a magnet, **every piece again has a north and a south pole**.

**The Earth is a magnet.** At the **geographic North Pole** there is a **magnetic south pole**. That is why the north pole of the compass needle points north. The magnetic axis is tilted a little against the Earth’s axis, so the compass deviates slightly from true north. Also, in Central Europe the field lines point down into the ground at a slant. A freely turning needle dips by about $65°$ to $68°$ in Germany (**inclination**), not at all at the equator and vertically at the magnetic poles.

## Exercises

### Exercise 1: Recognising poles

A magnet hangs on a thread and turns until one end points north. Is this end a north or a south pole? Why?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

It is a **north pole**. It is attracted by the Earth’s magnetic south pole, which lies near the geographic North Pole. That is where the north pole gets its name: it is the pole that “points north”. [Show the Earth](?mode=earth&lat=51&tilt=10&lines=1)

</details>

### Exercise 2: Magnet or iron?

You have two rods that look the same. One is a magnet, the other just iron. How can you find out which is the magnet without any other aids?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

Hold the end of one rod to the **middle** of the other. In the middle of a magnet the force is very small. If rod A strongly attracts the middle of rod B, A is the magnet. If hardly anything happens, B is the magnet. By the way, repulsion only happens between two magnets: if something repels, both are magnets. [Iron near the magnet](?mode=poles&other=iron&bx=5)

</details>

### Exercise 3: A broken magnet

A bar magnet breaks in the middle. Do you get a single north pole and a single south pole this way?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

No. Every piece is again a complete magnet with a north and a south pole. Elementary magnets explain this: in the broken piece, too, all the small magnets point in the same direction. [Break it](?mode=domains&pieces=2)

</details>

### Exercise 4: A compass next to the magnet

A compass lies to the left of the south pole of a bar magnet. Where does the red tip of the needle point?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

Towards the **south pole of the magnet**, i.e. to the right. The north pole of the needle is attracted by the south pole. [Show the compass](?mode=field&arr=single&rot=0&lines=1&filings=0&needles=0&cx=-7&cy=0)

</details>

## Ideas for the classroom

- **Getting started:** before opening the simulation, let the class sort a box of everyday objects and then compare with the game. The 1-cent coin is often a surprise.
- **Drawing field lines:** drag the compass step by step around the magnet and copy the directions of the needle onto paper. This way the field line pattern emerges by itself.
- **Questioning the model:** elementary magnets are a model. Discuss together what it explains well (magnetising, breaking, heating) and where it stops.
- **Common pitfalls:** “All metals are magnetic”; “The Earth’s North Pole is a magnetic north pole”; “A broken magnet has only one pole left”.
- **Curriculum link:** permanent magnets usually appear in grades 5 to 7. Inclination is an extension (please check your own curriculum).
