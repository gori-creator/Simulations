## What is it about?

A rectangle has a **length** $a$ and a **width** $b$. Two questions sound similar but mean quite different things:

**Area $A$ – how much surface does the rectangle cover?** We measure it with **unit squares**. A square with side $1\,\text{cm}$ has an area of $1\,\text{cm}^2$ (one *square centimetre*). A rectangle with $a = 5\,\text{cm}$ and $b = 3\,\text{cm}$ holds $3$ rows of $5$ unit squares each, so $5 \cdot 3 = 15$ squares:

$$
A = a \cdot b \qquad\text{e.g.}\quad A = 5\,\text{cm} \cdot 3\,\text{cm} = 15\,\text{cm}^2
$$

**Perimeter $P$ – how long is the edge all the way round?** The perimeter is a **length** and is given in $\text{cm}$. Walk along the four sides: $a + b + a + b$. Opposite sides are equally long, so

$$
P = 2 \cdot a + 2 \cdot b = 2 \cdot (a + b) \qquad\text{e.g.}\quad P = 2 \cdot (5\,\text{cm} + 3\,\text{cm}) = 16\,\text{cm}
$$

If you unroll the edge like a string, you get two equal pieces of length $a + b$ – that is where the bracket form $2 \cdot (a + b)$ comes from.

For a **square**, all sides are equally long: $A = a \cdot a$ and $P = 4 \cdot a$.

**Same perimeter – different area.** All rectangles with $P = 16\,\text{cm}$ have $a + b = 8\,\text{cm}$:

| Rectangle | $1 \times 7$ | $2 \times 6$ | $3 \times 5$ | $4 \times 4$ | $5 \times 3$ | $6 \times 2$ | $7 \times 1$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $A$ in $\text{cm}^2$ | $7$ | $12$ | $15$ | $16$ | $15$ | $12$ | $7$ |

The more "square" the rectangle, the larger its area; the square has the largest. The other way round, with a fixed area long thin strips have the largest perimeter: for $A = 12\,\text{cm}^2$, $1\,\text{cm} \times 12\,\text{cm}$ has a perimeter of $26\,\text{cm}$, $3\,\text{cm} \times 4\,\text{cm}$ only $14\,\text{cm}$.

**Composite shapes** such as an L-shape can be split into rectangles ($A = A_1 + A_2$) or completed to a big rectangle, subtracting the missing piece ($A = a \cdot b - c \cdot d$). Surprisingly, the perimeter of the L-shape is exactly the same as that of the big rectangle.

### Task 1: Area and perimeter

A rectangle is $8\,\text{cm}$ long and $3\,\text{cm}$ wide. Work out its area and perimeter. [Load task](?a=8&b=3&show=0&_hide=1)

$A = 8\,\text{cm} \cdot 3\,\text{cm} = 24\,\text{cm}^2$ and $P = 2 \cdot (8\,\text{cm} + 3\,\text{cm}) = 2 \cdot 11\,\text{cm} = 22\,\text{cm}$. [Show in the simulation](?a=8&b=3)

### Task 2: Working backwards

(a) A square has a perimeter of $20\,\text{cm}$. How long is one side, and what is its area?
(b) A rectangle has an area of $18\,\text{cm}^2$ and is $6\,\text{cm}$ long. How wide is it, and what is its perimeter?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

(a) $4 \cdot a = 20\,\text{cm}$, so $a = 5\,\text{cm}$ and $A = 5\,\text{cm} \cdot 5\,\text{cm} = 25\,\text{cm}^2$. [Show](?a=5&b=5)

(b) $6\,\text{cm} \cdot b = 18\,\text{cm}^2$, so $b = 3\,\text{cm}$ and $P = 2 \cdot (6\,\text{cm} + 3\,\text{cm}) = 18\,\text{cm}$. [Show](?a=6&b=3)

</details>

### Task 3: True or false?

Lena says: "A rectangle with a larger perimeter also has a larger area." Is she right?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

No. Counterexample: the strip $10\,\text{cm} \times 1\,\text{cm}$ has $P = 22\,\text{cm}$ but only $A = 10\,\text{cm}^2$. The square $4\,\text{cm} \times 4\,\text{cm}$ has the smaller perimeter $16\,\text{cm}$ but the larger area $16\,\text{cm}^2$. [Strip](?a=10&b=1) · [Square](?a=4&b=4)

</details>

### Task 4: The largest flower bed

You have $24\,\text{m}$ of fencing for a rectangular flower bed. The sides should be whole metres. How long and how wide must the bed be so that it is as large as possible? (In the simulation, use $1\,\text{cm}$ for $1\,\text{m}$.) [Load task](?mode=gleichU&u=24&w=1&_hide=1)

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

$a + b = 12\,\text{m}$. The area is largest for the square $6\,\text{m} \times 6\,\text{m}$: $A = 36\,\text{m}^2$. For comparison: $5\,\text{m} \times 7\,\text{m}$ gives only $35\,\text{m}^2$, $1\,\text{m} \times 11\,\text{m}$ only $11\,\text{m}^2$. [Show](?mode=gleichU&u=24&w=6)

</details>

### Task 5: L-shape in two ways

A $3\,\text{cm} \times 4\,\text{cm}$ piece is cut from the top right corner of an $8\,\text{cm} \times 6\,\text{cm}$ rectangle. Find the area in two different ways, and the perimeter. [Load task](?mode=lform&a=8&b=6&c=3&d=4&show=0&_hide=1)

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

Split across: $8 \cdot 2 + 5 \cdot 4 = 16 + 20 = 36$, so $A = 36\,\text{cm}^2$. Completed: $8 \cdot 6 - 3 \cdot 4 = 48 - 12 = 36$. [Split](?mode=lform&a=8&b=6&c=3&d=4&zerl=waag) · [Complete](?mode=lform&a=8&b=6&c=3&d=4&zerl=erg)

Perimeter: $8 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 = 28$, so $P = 28\,\text{cm} = 2 \cdot (8\,\text{cm} + 6\,\text{cm})$ – the same as for the whole rectangle. [Push the edges](?mode=lform&a=8&b=6&c=3&d=4&kanten=1)

</details>

## Ideas for the classroom

- **Use:** Grade 5, quantities and their units, section on area. Suitable for introducing the topic (tiling, the ant), for practice (task links with `show=0&_hide=1`) and for going further (comparisons, L-shape).
- **Model:** side lengths are whole centimetres ($a \le 10$, $b \le 6$). The squared paper shows whole centimetres in bold and half centimetres in fine lines – as in German exercise books, where one small square is $5\,\text{mm}$ wide. There, one square centimetre consists of **four** small squares. On screen, "1 cm" is of course not really 1 cm long.
- **Representations:** length $a$ (horizontal) and width $b$ (vertical) have the same colour in the picture, in the formulas and in the string. The string falls into two pieces of length $a + b$ and so justifies $P = 2 \cdot (a + b)$. In German, the perimeter is written $U$ (for *Umfang*).
- **Common stumbling blocks:**
  - "You can compare perimeter and area" – for the $4 \times 4$ square the numbers are equal, but the quantities (length and area) are not.
  - "A larger perimeter means a larger area" – see the *Same perimeter* mode.
  - Swapping the formulas ($P = a \cdot b$, $A = a + b$) or adding only two sides for the perimeter ($P = a + b$).
  - For the L-shape, the two inner edges are forgotten in the perimeter, or $A = a \cdot b$ is used.
  - On squared paper, every small square is counted as $1\,\text{cm}^2$.
- **Outlook:** converting units of area ($1\,\text{cm}^2 = 100\,\text{mm}^2$) and the area of parallelograms, triangles and trapeziums by splitting and completing in Year 6.
