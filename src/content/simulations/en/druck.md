## What is it about?

**Pressure** tells how strongly a force acts on an area: $p = \frac{F}{A}$, unit $1\,\text{Pa} = 1\,\text{N}/\text{m}^2$; also $1\,\text{hPa} = 100\,\text{Pa}$ and $1\,\text{bar} = 100\,000\,\text{Pa}$.

### Force on a large or small area

The same weight produces a large pressure on a small area. A stiletto heel presses harder into the ground than an elephant's foot; snowshoes and skis spread the weight. Knives, needles and drawing pins use small areas on purpose.

### Hydraulic press

In an enclosed liquid the pressure is **the same everywhere**. Pushing with $F_1$ on a small piston gives $F_2 = F_1 \cdot \frac{A_2}{A_1}$ on the large one – but the large piston moves only a fraction of the distance. Car jacks, excavators and brakes work this way.

### Pressure in water

The water above a diver presses with its weight: $p = \rho \cdot g \cdot h$. Every 10 m of depth adds about 1 bar, acting from **all sides**.

### Connected vessels and water tower

Vessels of different shapes filled to the same height have the **same pressure at the bottom** – whether a narrow tube, a bucket or a wide bowl. That is surprising, because the bowl holds far more water. It is called the **hydrostatic paradox**: only the height $h$ of the water column counts, $p = \rho \cdot g \cdot h$.

That is why a **water tower** stands high up. Its water level sets the pressure in all pipes: a tap 30 m lower has an overpressure of about 3 bar. The higher the floor, the weaker the jet – above the water level nothing comes out. Tall buildings therefore need their own pumps.

### Air pressure

Air has weight too: about $1013\,\text{hPa}$ at sea level, half that at about 5.5 km.

### Measuring pressure: barometer and Magdeburg hemispheres

In 1643 Evangelista Torricelli filled a glass tube with mercury and stood it upside down in a dish. The column fell to about **760 mm** with almost a vacuum above it. Air pressure on the dish holds the column: $p = \rho \cdot g \cdot h$. With water the tube would have to be over **10 m** long.

In 1656 Otto von Guericke pumped the air out of two hemispheres placed together. Two teams of horses could not pull them apart: air pressure pushes with $F = \Delta p \cdot \pi \cdot r^2$ – for $r = 25\,\text{cm}$ about **20,000 N**, the weight of two tonnes. The circle area counts, not the surface of the sphere.

### Wind as pressure balance

Over warmer ground air rises and a **low** forms at the surface; air flows in from the **high**. That is why the coast has a sea breeze by day and a land breeze at night.

### Everyday: drinking through a straw

When you “suck” on a straw you do not pull the drink up. You enlarge your mouth and so lower the pressure inside. The outside air pressure then pushes the liquid up the straw – until the weight pressure of the column balances the underpressure: h = Δp / (ρ · g). With about 100 hPa of underpressure you manage roughly one metre of water. Even a perfect pump can lift water by suction by at most about 10 m.

### Everyday: blood pressure in the body

The blood in the vessels also forms a column of liquid. When standing, the blood pressure at the feet is about 100 mmHg higher than at the heart, and a little lower in the head. That is why blood pressure is measured at the upper arm, roughly at heart level. The unit mmHg comes from the mercury barometer: 1 mmHg ≈ 133 Pa.

## Exercises

**1. High heels.** A person (700 N) stands on two heels with 4 cm² in total. Pressure? Compare with trainers (300 cm²). [Load exercise](?mode=auflage&F=700&flaeche=absatz&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

$1\,750\,000\,\text{Pa} = 17.5\,\text{bar}$; with trainers about 23 kPa – 75 times less.

</details>

**2. Car jack.** 100 N on a 2 cm piston, the large piston has 20 cm diameter. Force there? [Load exercise](?mode=hydraulik&F1=100&d1=2&d2=20&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

100 times the area, so $F_2 = 10\,000\,\text{N}$.

</details>

**3. Diving.** Pressure at 25 m depth in fresh water? [Load exercise](?mode=wasser&h=25&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

About $1.013 + 2.45 \approx 3.47\,\text{bar}$.

</details>

**4. On the mountain.** Air pressure on the Zugspitze (2962 m)? [Load exercise](?mode=luft&hoehe=2962&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

About 712 hPa, roughly 70 % of sea level; water boils at about 90 °C there.

</details>

**5. Water tower.** The water level of a water tower is 40 m above the ground. What is the overpressure at a tap on the ground floor, and on the 3rd floor (10 m up)? [Load task](?mode=gefaesse&gv=turm&th=40&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Ground floor: $p = 1000 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 40\,\text{Pa} \approx 3.9\,\text{bar}$. 3rd floor: $1000 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 30\,\text{Pa} \approx 2.9\,\text{bar}$.

</details>

**6. Magdeburg hemispheres.** Hemispheres with $r = 25\,\text{cm}$ are pumped empty. With what force does air pressure ($1000\,\text{hPa}$) press them together? [Load task](?mode=messen&mv=magde&r=25&pin=0&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

$A = \pi \cdot (0.25\,\text{m})^2 \approx 0.196\,\text{m}^2$, $F = 100\,000\,\text{Pa} \cdot 0.196\,\text{m}^2 \approx 19\,600\,\text{N}$.

</details>

## Ideas for teaching

- Connect two syringes of different sizes with a tube and compare the forces.
- Why do your ears pop when diving or flying? Why are dams thicker at the bottom?
- Fill a glass to the brim with water, put a postcard on top and turn it over – air pressure holds the card.
- Common mistake: more water does not mean more pressure at the bottom – only the height counts.
