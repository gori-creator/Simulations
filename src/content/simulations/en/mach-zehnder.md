## What is it about?

In a **Mach-Zehnder interferometer** a half-silvered mirror (**beam splitter**) divides light into two paths. Two mirrors guide both parts to a second beam splitter, where they meet again. Two detectors sit behind it.

### With laser light

The two partial waves **interfere**. With equal paths all light reaches detector 1; a phase shift of half a wavelength (180°) sends it all to detector 2: $P_1 = \cos^2(\Delta\varphi/2)$, $P_2 = \sin^2(\Delta\varphi/2)$.

### With single photons

Each photon is registered at **only one** detector – it does not split. Which detector clicks is **random**; only after many photons does the laser distribution appear. With equal paths **only** detector 1 clicks. As long as the path **cannot in principle be determined**, both possibilities superpose and interfere.

### Which-path information

Marking the paths (e.g. with crossed polarising filters) makes the path knowable – and the **interference disappears**: each detector gets half. This is **complementarity**. Removing the second beam splitter also kills the interference; it can even be done while the photon is on its way (**delayed choice**).

## Exercises

**1. Prediction.** At 90°, what is the probability for detector 1? Can you predict a single photon? [Load exercise](?phase=90&bs2=1&mark=0&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

50 %; only the probability can be predicted, not the single result.

</details>

**2. Does the photon split?** Why do "both paths" matter even though only one detector clicks? [Load exercise](?phase=0&bs2=1&mark=0&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

With equal paths **all** photons reach detector 1 – impossible if each took only one path (that would give 50 : 50).

</details>

**3. Polarising filters.** Crossed filters in the two paths. Result? [Load exercise](?phase=0&bs2=1&mark=1&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

50 % each – the path is knowable, so there is no interference.

</details>

**4. Laser vs single photons.** What is the same, what differs?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

The **distribution** is the same; with single photons it builds up from random single events.

</details>
