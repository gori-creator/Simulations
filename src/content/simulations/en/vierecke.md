## What is it about?

A **quadrilateral** $ABCD$ has sides $a = AB$, $b = BC$, $c = CD$, $d = DA$, interior angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$ with $\alpha + \beta + \gamma + \delta = 360^\circ$, and diagonals $e = AC$, $f = BD$. Special quadrilaterals are defined by their sides, angles and **symmetry** (inclusive definitions, as in Bavarian schools):

| Type | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| trapezium (US: trapezoid) | at least one pair of parallel opposite sides |
| isosceles trapezium | trapezium with a line of symmetry through the midpoints of the parallel sides |
| kite | symmetric about a diagonal |
| parallelogram | both pairs of opposite sides parallel (point symmetry) |
| rhombus | four equal sides |
| rectangle | four right angles |
| square | rhombus and rectangle |

In the **house of quadrilaterals** an arrow from X to Y means: X is a special case of Y, so every X is also a Y. Every square is a rectangle – but not every rectangle is a square. Marks: arrows = parallel, ticks = equal length, quarter circle with dot = right angle, dash-dot line = line of symmetry. One grid square is $0.5\,\text{cm}$.

## Notes for teachers

- Grades 5 and 7: describing special quadrilaterals and ordering them by symmetry. Corners snap to grid points, so all properties are detected exactly; crossed quadrilaterals are not allowed.
- Inclusive definitions; an isosceles trapezium is an axially symmetric trapezium, and non-convex kites count as kites. Check against your textbook.
- Common misconceptions: “a square is not a rectangle”, “a square standing on a corner is a rhombus, not a square”, “a diagonal of a parallelogram is a line of symmetry”.
