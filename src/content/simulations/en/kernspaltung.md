## What is it about?

Nuclei consist of protons and neutrons (**nucleons**). The strong force binds them so tightly that a nucleus is **lighter** than its parts – the **mass defect**, which by $E = mc^2$ equals the **binding energy** $B$.

### The binding-energy curve

$B/A$ rises steeply for light nuclei, peaks at **iron** (about 8.8 MeV) and falls slowly for heavy nuclei. Hence:

- **Fission:** a heavy nucleus such as U-235 splits into two medium nuclei that are more tightly bound – about 200 MeV per fission is released.
- **Fusion:** very light nuclei fuse (D + T → He-4); per nucleon even more energy is released. This powers the Sun.

Burning coal releases only a few eV per molecule – millions of times less.

### Chain reaction

A U-235 nucleus capturing a neutron splits and releases **2–3 neutrons**. The **multiplication factor** $k$ decides: $k < 1$ subcritical (dies out), $k = 1$ critical (power plant), $k > 1$ supercritical (exponential growth). **Control rods** absorb neutrons to regulate $k$.

### One fission in detail

A slow neutron is captured by a U-235 nucleus. The new nucleus U-236 is excited: it oscillates, stretches into a dumbbell and bursts into two medium-sized fragments, for example barium-141 and krypton-92, plus 2 to 3 neutrons. The number of nucleons (236) and the number of protons (92) are conserved. About 200 MeV are released, almost all as kinetic energy of the fragments.

### Mass defect: E = m · c²

If you weigh all particles before and after the reaction, the mass afterwards is slightly smaller. This **mass defect** $\Delta m$ has been released as energy: $E = \Delta m \cdot c^2$. In the fission of uranium about 0.08 % of the mass disappears, in fusion in the Sun about 0.7 %. The Sun becomes about 4 million tonnes lighter every second.

### Power plant: uranium or coal?

Completely splitting one gram of uranium-235 gives about 23 MWh of heat – as much as almost 3 tonnes of hard coal. In the power plant this heat boils water, and the steam drives a turbine with a generator. A large unit splits only a few kilograms of uranium per day. In return it produces radioactive fission products that must be stored safely for a very long time.

### The moderator

Fission neutrons are very fast (about 2 MeV). U-235, however, splits much more easily with **slow** (thermal) neutrons, so a **moderator** slows them down. As in billiards, the neutron loses most energy when it hits a partner of the same mass: in water (hydrogen nuclei) about 18 collisions are enough, in graphite it takes around 115, and it bounces off heavy lead nuclei with hardly any loss.

### Radioactive waste

The fission products are radioactive. Whether a substance disappears quickly depends on its **half-life**: iodine-131 (8 days) is almost gone after a few months, caesium-137 and strontium-90 (about 30 years) stay radioactive for centuries, plutonium-239 (24 100 years) for tens of thousands of years. That is why a safe final repository is needed.

## Exercises

**1. Fission energy.** U-235: about 7.6 MeV per nucleon, fragments about 8.5 MeV. Estimate the energy released. [Load exercise](?mode=bindung&A=235&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

About $235 \cdot 0.9\,\text{MeV} \approx 210\,\text{MeV}$ (measured about 200 MeV).

</details>

**2. Why not iron?** Can energy be gained from iron-56? [Load exercise](?mode=bindung&A=56&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

No – iron is at the maximum of the curve.

</details>

**3. Critical reactor.** 2.5 neutrons per fission, 30 % escape. What share must the control rods absorb for $k = 1$? [Load exercise](?mode=kette&stab=40&leck=30&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

$2.5 \cdot 0.7 \cdot (1 - x) = 1$, so about **43 %**.

</details>

**4. Avalanche.** With $k = 2$, starting from one neutron, how many after 10 generations?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

$2^{10} = 1024$ – within about $10^{-7}\,\text{s}$ in a bomb.

</details>

**Fission products.** U-235 + n → Cs-137 + Rb-? + 2 n. What is the mass number of the rubidium, and how many protons does it have? [Load task](?mode=spaltung&ch=csRb&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

$236 - 137 - 2 = 97$, so Rb-97. Caesium has 55 protons, leaving $92 - 55 = 37$ for rubidium.

</details>
