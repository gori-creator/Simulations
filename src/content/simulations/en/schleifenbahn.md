## What is it about?

Planets slowly wander across the starry sky – mostly from west to east. But Mars, Jupiter and Saturn sometimes stop, move **backwards** for a few weeks and then forwards again. A **loop** appears in the sky. For ancient astronomers this was a great puzzle.

### The explanation: overtaking on the inside track

Earth orbits the Sun faster than the outer planets. When it **overtakes** Mars on the inside track, Mars – like a slower car on the motorway – seems to fall back against the distant background. The dashed line of sight in the simulation shows where we are looking.

Because the planets’ orbits are slightly tilted against Earth’s orbit, they do not run back along exactly the same line: a loop or a zigzag forms. (The height is exaggerated in the simulation so it is easier to see.)

### Opposition and visibility

In the middle of its backward motion an outer planet stands **opposite** the Sun (**opposition**). Then it

- rises at sunset and is visible **all night**,
- is closest to Earth and especially bright.

A planet close to the Sun (small **elongation**) is lost in the Sun’s glare. If it is east of the Sun, you see it **in the evening sky**; west of it, **in the morning sky**.

**Venus** orbits inside Earth’s orbit and faster than Earth. It never gets more than about 47° from the Sun – so it is only seen as the “evening star” or “morning star”, never at midnight. It becomes retrograde when it overtakes Earth between Earth and the Sun.

### Geocentric or heliocentric?

Ptolemy explained the loops with circles on circles (epicycles) around a resting Earth. Copernicus showed that they arise much more simply if all planets orbit the Sun – a step towards the **heliocentric world view**.

How often the loop repeats is set by the **synodic period**: for Mars about every 2.1 years, for Jupiter about every 13 months.

## Exercises

**1. Midnight.** Why can Mars be seen high in the south at midnight at opposition, but Venus never? [Load exercise](?planet=venus)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

At midnight you look exactly away from the Sun. At opposition Mars is there. Venus orbits inside Earth’s orbit and is never more than about 47° from the Sun.

</details>

**2. Synodic period.** Using $\tfrac{1}{S} = 1 - \tfrac{1}{T}$, calculate the time between two oppositions of Mars ($T = 1.88$ years). [Load exercise](?planet=mars)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

$\tfrac{1}{S} = 1 - \tfrac{1}{1.88} \approx 0.468$, so $S \approx 2.14$ years – about 2 years and 7 weeks.

</details>
