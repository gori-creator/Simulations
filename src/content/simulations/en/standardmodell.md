## What is it about?

What is the world made of? According to current knowledge, of surprisingly few **elementary particles**. The **Standard Model of particle physics** organises them and describes how they act on each other.

### Matter particles: quarks and leptons

There are six **quarks** and six **leptons**, each in three **generations**. Everything around us in everyday life is made only of the first generation:

- The **up quark** (charge $+\tfrac23 e$) and the **down quark** ($-\tfrac13 e$) form protons and neutrons.
- The **electron** surrounds the nucleus.
- The **electron neutrino** is produced in beta decay; it is almost massless and passes through the whole Earth.

The second and third generations are heavier copies. They only appear at high energies (in accelerators or from cosmic rays) and decay quickly. The **top quark** is about as heavy as a whole gold atom.

Every particle has an **antiparticle** with the same mass and opposite charge.

### Force carriers

Forces arise from the exchange of particles:

- **photon** – electromagnetic interaction (holds atoms together),
- **gluons** – strong interaction (holds quarks together),
- **W and Z bosons** – weak interaction (beta decay, fusion in the Sun).

The **Higgs boson** (discovered at CERN in 2012) belongs to the Higgs field, which gives particles their mass. Gravity is not part of the Standard Model – one of the big open questions.

### Hadrons from quarks

Quarks never occur alone (**confinement**). They always form particles with a **whole-number charge**:

- **baryons** from three quarks, e.g. the proton $uud$: $\tfrac23 + \tfrac23 - \tfrac13 = +1$, the neutron $udd$: $0$.
- **mesons** from a quark and an antiquark, e.g. the pion $\pi^+ = u\bar d$.

Quarks also carry a **colour charge** (red, green, blue – just a name, not a real colour). A baryon contains all three colours, a meson a colour and the matching anticolour; from the outside every hadron is “white”. In the 1950s hundreds of such particles were found – the **particle zoo**. The quark model brought order to it.

### Pair production

A photon with enough energy can turn into an **electron and a positron** near a nucleus – energy becomes mass ($E = mc^2$). This needs at least

$$E_\gamma \geq 2 \cdot m_e c^2 = 1.022\,\text{MeV}.$$

The rest becomes kinetic energy. In a bubble chamber with a magnetic field, the electron and positron move in **opposite spirals** because of their opposite charges; the spirals get tighter as the particles lose energy. The photon itself leaves no track.

## Exercises

**1. Charge of the omega.** The $\Omega^-$ consists of three strange quarks. Calculate its charge. [Load exercise](?mode=baukasten&q1=s&q2=s&q3=s&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

$3 \cdot \left(-\tfrac13\right) = -1$. Gell-Mann predicted the particle in 1962 using the quark model; two years later it was found.

</details>

**2. Not allowed.** Why is there no particle made of two up quarks? [Load exercise](?mode=baukasten&q1=u&q2=u&q3=0&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

The charge would be $+\tfrac43$ – not a whole number. Also, two colours cannot combine to “white”.

</details>

**3. Pair production.** A 4 MeV photon creates an electron–positron pair. How much kinetic energy does each particle get if they share the energy equally? [Load exercise](?mode=paar&E=4&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

$\dfrac{4 - 1.022}{2}\,\text{MeV} \approx 1.49$ MeV.

</details>
