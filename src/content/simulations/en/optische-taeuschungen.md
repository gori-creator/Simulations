## What is it about?

Our eyes and brain are very good at interpreting pictures quickly. To do that they take shortcuts, and sometimes these lead us astray. **Optical illusions** show that lengths, sizes and angles cannot always be judged by eye. That is why in mathematics we **measure** instead of relying on our impression.

### Müller-Lyer illusion

Two segments of equal length are given arrowheads at their ends, one pointing inwards and one outwards. The segment with the outward-opening fins looks clearly **longer**. One explanation is that the brain reads the fins like the corners of a room, once near and once far, and "corrects" the size accordingly.

### Ebbinghaus illusion

Two circles of equal size look different. The circle surrounded by large circles looks **smaller**, and the one surrounded by small circles looks **larger**. We always judge sizes in comparison with their surroundings.

### Zöllner illusion

Long, exactly **parallel** lines are given short slanted strokes, alternating in one direction and the other. As a result the long lines seem to run apart or together.

### Hering illusion

Two exactly **straight** lines in front of a fan of rays appear to bend outwards.

### Impossible figures and tiling puzzles

In **impossible figures** such as the Penrose triangle or the endless staircase, the parts fit together in the picture, but you could never build them like that in space. In **tiling puzzles** such as the tangram, you put together different figures from the same pieces. Their area always stays the same, even though they look completely different.

**2. Parallel?** How can you prove that the long lines in the Zöllner illusion are parallel? [Load exercise](?mode=zoellner&w=45&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Measure the distance between two lines at two places, perpendicular to the lines. If it is the same, they are parallel. You can also measure the angle to the edge of the picture with a set square.

</details>

**3. Explaining.** Why does the middle circle surrounded by small circles look larger? [Load exercise](?mode=ebbinghaus&set=100&_hide=1)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

We judge sizes in comparison with the surroundings. Next to small circles the circle looks large, and next to large circles it looks small.

</details>
